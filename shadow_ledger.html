Questions you should ask

Straight answers.

We're not in the EU. Does the AI Act even apply to us?

Maybe — the Act reaches providers and deployers whose output is used in the EU, and your clients, acquirers, or insurers may apply its standard contractually long before a regulator does. But the honest framing is broader: the ledger is an exposure map, not just a regulation map. The $4.63M breach number doesn't check your jurisdiction before it lands. If your exposure is genuinely zero, the memo is a clean bill of health — and you'll want that in writing eventually anyway.

Will employees get in trouble?

No — and structurally so. The survey is anonymous and aggregated; no deliverable names an individual. The product is exposure mapping, not surveillance: the whole method depends on honest answers, and honest answers depend on consequences being impossible. What the board receives is "the finance team routes contracts through tool X," never "Sarah does." Culture questions stay cultural; the ledger handles the factual ones.

What if we find nothing?

Then you've bought the cheapest clean bill of health on the market — a documented, evidence-backed memo that your AI surface is inventoried and controlled, dated and signed. That document has a way of becoming valuable the week an acquirer, insurer, or enterprise client sends a diligence questionnaire. And in the research's 55–78% range, "nothing" is the rare outcome; finding something is the base case, and finding it before an incident is the entire point.

What access do you need from IT?

Read-level cooperation, about three hours of IT time across the week: the extension and app telemetry your MDM or browser management already collects, the SaaS-wallet export from finance, and expense records. No endpoint agents, no deployment, no credentials stored. If IT can't assist some days, the survey and expense scans still run — the ledger just flags which routes carry wider confidence intervals.

How is this different from our security team's tooling?

Security tooling watches the network and the endpoints — it sees traffic, not intent, retention terms, or process. The ledger grades what the tools can't: that the "free transcription app" processes meetings under consumer terms, that the AI feature your CRM vendor enabled shares data per its updated policy, that the extension reads every page your team opens. Instrumentation tells you something is running; the ledger tells you what it means, in risk classes, with a remediation order.

Why 7 days — and why only one organization at a time?

Because the method is interview-heavy: the anonymous survey needs a real response window, the discovery cross-check needs finance and IT in the loop, and the grading needs the founder reading every usage path personally. Capping at one ledger per week is what keeps the memo board-grade instead of template-grade. Larger organizations split into entities or business units — same per-unit price, sequenced windows.

Can the policy be bilingual — and does the GCC variant exist?

Yes. The one-page policy ships in an English/Arabic variant at no extra cost, and the grading framework has a GCC regional variant that maps exposure against the applicable regional frameworks (Saudi PDPL-adjacent data rules, UAE data protection law) alongside the EU classes where relevant. The bilingual deliverable is often the one that actually changes behavior — a policy your whole workforce can read is a policy your whole workforce can follow.
